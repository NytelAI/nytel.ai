What counts as proof

One trial wins. If you ran it again, would it win again?

Try an example:

1. First trial result

Win line: p below 0.05, counting luck in both directions (two-sided). A useless drug wins on the good side 1 time in 40.

What the picture shows

Each bar counts pretend second trials of the same drug, made by a computer. Teal bars win, orange bars do not.

The formula curve is the bell shape that chance alone produces, so it matches the bars.

Assumes the true effect (how much better the drug really is, its effect size) equals what trial 1 saw. The chance that a trial finds a real effect is its power; real effects are often smaller, so real odds are lower.

Each dot is a useless drug. Orange dots got lucky and "won". Pick a rule.

Predict, then check

A marker is a stand-in for how patients really do; deaths are a hard endpoint.

How the rule changed

Faster is not easier

FDA can speed up the review of serious diseases. It does not lower the proof.

Real cases: when "it helps" was wrong

Thalidomide (1950s to 1962). An estimated 10,000 babies were born with birth defects. FDA's Frances Kelsey had refused to approve it.

Congress then passed the Kefauver-Harris Amendments of 1962: show the drug works, with adequate and well-controlled trials.

CAST (late 1980s). Heart drugs fixed a rhythm marker on a heart monitor, but patients died more than on placebo. The trial stopped early.

Sources: Sokolova and Sokolov (2026), Clinical Trials in Practice, chapters 1 (history), 17 (endpoints) and 16 (randomization); Rossouw et al., Women's Health Initiative, JAMA 2002; CAST Investigators, N Engl J Med 1989. The book gives no counts for CAST.

Regulator's corner

"Substantial evidence" is the law's phrase for this page's question: adequate, well-controlled trials. FDA's draft guidance (June 2026) says one trial can be enough if it comes with:

"a highly persuasive trial or a source of strong confirmatory evidence"

Confirmatory evidence means other data that backs up a single trial.

FDA draft guidance, Demonstrating Substantial Evidence of Effectiveness for Human Drug and Biological Products. It is a draft (Section IV.A). In our lab a very small p-value is part of "highly persuasive"; the guidance also weighs meaning for patients, trial quality and consistency across subgroups.

Takeaway

  • A trial that barely wins repeats only about half the time, if its result is the true effect.
  • Two wins by luck are rare (1 in 1,600). One very strong win plus other evidence can also count.
  • A fast review is not a weaker standard. A marker that improves is not proof that patients do better.
