Fishing in many comparisons

The drug does nothing, but you test it in several groups of patients. Each dot is one test.

Orange dot = a false alarm (p below 0.05). The tests are made by computer.

p = 0.05 is not "5% chance the null is true"

Orange: the p-value. Teal: the smallest chance that luck alone (the null) could still explain the result, given that p-value.

This assumes a 50:50 chance before the trial. The real chance is usually even higher. Source: Berger table in Bayes, AI and Deep Learning, chapter 6.

A real example: EOLIA

EOLIA tested ECMO (a machine that does the lungs' work) against usual care in severe lung failure. The p-value for 60-day deaths was 0.09, so the trial was called "not significant".

A later reanalysis that starts from a prior (module 1) put the chance that ECMO lowers deaths between 88% and 99%, depending on the prior. "Not significant" did not mean "no effect".

Sources: Combes A. et al. (2018), N Engl J Med 378:1965-1975 (EOLIA). Goligher E.C. et al. (2018), JAMA 320:2251-2259 (reanalysis with priors).

Regulator's corner

Why do trials write the main outcome down before they see any data?

"To avoid multiplicity concerns arising from post hoc definitions, it is critical to specify in the protocol the precise definition of the primary variable ..."

Plain words: the protocol is the trial's written plan, fixed before it starts. Post hoc means chosen after seeing the data, and multiplicity means running many tests, as in Fishing above.

ICH (International Council for Harmonisation) is the group of regulators and industry that writes trial rules shared by the US, Europe and Japan.

ICH E9, Statistical Principles for Clinical Trials, section 2.2.2. Read the guideline

Takeaway

  • A p-value asks: if nothing is going on, how often would luck look this good?
  • It is not the chance that the drug works, and it depends on the plan you had before you looked.
  • Peeking and fishing make luck look like a discovery.
